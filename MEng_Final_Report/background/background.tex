\section{From Language Models to Agentic Systems}
\label{sec:bg-llms-to-agents}

Modern large language models are built on the Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. as a sequence model based entirely on attention rather than recurrence or convolution \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.40\textwidth]{figures/transformer_architecture.png}
  \caption{The Transformer encoder--decoder architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}.}
  \label{fig:transformer-architecture}
\end{figure}

The central mechanism is self-attention: for each token, the model learns to weight other tokens in the sequence according to their relevance. This made it possible to train large models efficiently on massive text corpora and to capture long-range dependencies that earlier recurrent models struggled to preserve. For this report, the important point is not the internal mathematics of attention itself, but the way that Transformer scaling produced models that can be treated as general-purpose language interfaces. Once a model can interpret a natural-language instruction, generate structured text, and maintain some conversational context, it can be embedded into a larger software system as a planner, controller, or decision-making component.

The GPT-3 work made this shift especially clear. Brown et al. showed that a sufficiently large pretrained language model could perform many tasks through in-context learning, using a prompt and a few examples rather than task-specific gradient updates \cite{brown2020language}. This changed the engineering model around language systems. Instead of training a separate classifier or sequence-to-sequence model for every task, developers could often specify a task through instructions, demonstrations, and formatting constraints. Instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback then made this interaction pattern more reliable, with Ouyang et al. showing that models trained to follow user instructions were preferred by human evaluators over much larger base models that had not received the same alignment process \cite{ouyang2022training}. These developments made LLMs attractive as building blocks for applications, but they also introduced a new reliability problem: much of the application's behaviour is now controlled by a probabilistic natural-language component rather than by conventional deterministic code.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/few_shot_prompt_example.png}
  \caption{Few-shot prompting: the model receives a task description and demonstration examples in context, then completes a new instance without weight updates \cite{brown2020language}.}
  \label{fig:few-shot-prompt}
\end{figure}

Prompting research further demonstrated that model behaviour can change substantially depending on how a task is framed. Few-shot prompting, in which a small set of input--output demonstrations is included in the prompt, lets the model infer the task from examples alone rather than from weight updates \cite{brown2020language}. Chain-of-thought prompting showed that asking a model to produce intermediate reasoning steps can improve performance on arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense tasks \cite{wei2022chain}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/chain_of_thought_prompting_example.png}
  \caption{Chain-of-thought prompting: intermediate reasoning steps are included in the prompt so the model can follow a similar decomposition at inference time \cite{wei2022chain}.}
  \label{fig:chain-of-thought}
\end{figure}

Kojima et al. showed that even a short zero-shot instruction such as asking the model to reason step by step can elicit similar behaviour in sufficiently capable models \cite{kojima2022large}. Wang et al. then introduced self-consistency, where several reasoning traces are sampled and the final answer is selected by agreement across traces \cite{wang2022self}. These papers are often discussed as prompt-engineering methods, but they are also important for evaluation. They show that the final answer is only one part of the behaviour: a model may take different internal or external paths to reach that answer, and repeated executions of the same task may not agree. The idea of evaluating a system over repeated trials therefore emerges naturally from the stochasticity and prompt sensitivity of LLMs.

As LLMs were incorporated into larger pipelines, retrieval-augmented generation became one of the most influential architectures. Lewis et al. proposed combining a parametric language model with a non-parametric retrieval component, so that the model could condition its generation on retrieved passages rather than relying only on information stored in its weights \cite{lewis2020retrieval}. RAG is important because it turns a single model call into a multi-component system: a query must be represented, documents must be retrieved, context must be selected, and the generator must use that context faithfully. This creates more opportunities for failure, but also creates more places to test. The retriever may return irrelevant information, the generator may ignore the retrieved evidence, or the final answer may be relevant but unsupported. Consequently, the evaluation of RAG systems already moves beyond checking the final string and toward checking the behaviour of interacting components.

The same shift is even clearer in tool-using agents. ReAct introduced a pattern in which an LLM interleaves reasoning traces with actions, such as search queries or environment commands \cite{yao2023react}. This allows the model to gather information, observe the result, and decide on the next step.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/react_prompting.png}
  \caption{ReAct prompting interleaves natural-language reasoning (\textit{Thought}) with executable actions (\textit{Act}) and environment observations (\textit{Obs}) \cite{yao2023react}.}
  \label{fig:react-prompting}
\end{figure}

Toolformer pushed the idea further by showing that language models can be trained to decide when to call external tools and how to use their results \cite{schick2023toolformer}. Gorilla similarly focused on connecting LLMs to large collections of APIs, evaluating whether the model could select and call the correct API from natural-language instructions \cite{patil2023gorilla}. These systems make the agent's execution trace a first-class object: the model's behaviour is no longer just a paragraph of generated text, but a sequence of decisions, tool calls, observations, and state changes.

This transition from text generation to agentic execution is the central background for the rest of this chapter. A conventional language benchmark asks whether a model can answer a question. A tool-using agent benchmark asks whether a model can pursue a goal in an environment. That distinction matters because the failure modes are different. An agent may choose the wrong tool, pass a subtly wrong argument, call tools in the wrong order, violate a domain policy, misread an observation, or leave the external environment in an invalid state. The final message may be polished and plausible while the underlying action sequence is wrong. Conversely, a final answer may be linguistically imperfect while the operational behaviour is correct. This is why recent work increasingly treats LLM agents as executable systems rather than as ordinary natural-language generation systems.

\section{Failure Modes in Tool-Using LLM Agents}
\label{sec:bg-agent-failures}

Hallucination remains the most widely discussed failure mode of LLMs. In a single-turn question-answering setting, hallucination usually means that the model states unsupported or false information with high confidence. In an agentic setting, the same phenomenon can manifest in more operationally dangerous ways. An agent can hallucinate the contents of a tool result, infer a state that the environment has not actually reached, or claim that an action has been completed when it has not. Because later steps may depend on earlier observations, a single hallucinated intermediate assumption can propagate through a trajectory and produce a final state that is difficult to diagnose by reading only the last response.

Tool use introduces several additional failure classes. First, an agent may fail at tool selection: it may call a search tool when a database lookup is required, or call a write action when it should only inspect state. Second, it may fail at argument construction: it may choose the right tool but provide the wrong identifier, date, quantity, or filter. Third, it may fail at sequencing: even correct individual calls may be wrong if made in the wrong order. Fourth, the agent may fail to abstain. In real systems, a safe agent often needs to ask for clarification, refuse to proceed, or stop after insufficient information rather than forcing a tool call. Function-calling benchmarks such as BFCL explicitly identify abstention and multi-step function-calling as important capabilities, illustrating that tool-use quality cannot be reduced to a single final answer \cite{patil2025bfcl}. ToolEmu systematically surfaces these patterns in an LM-emulated tool sandbox, showing that correct tool choice does not guarantee safe execution when arguments are wrong, fabricated, or misaligned with the instruction \cite{ruan2024toolemu}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/tool_emu_failure_examples.png}
  \caption{Typical failures of LM agents in ToolEmu \cite{ruan2024toolemu}: fabrication or unwarranted assumptions [(c), (d)], instruction misinterpretation [(c)], erroneous executions [(b)], and risk ignorance [(a), (e)]. Several panels show the right tool invoked with incorrect or unsafe arguments [(c), (d)]. Trajectories are simplified for illustration; risky actions are highlighted in red and notable issues in yellow.}
  \label{fig:toolemu-failures}
\end{figure}

Multi-turn interaction makes these issues more pronounced. AgentBench evaluates LLMs as agents across multiple interactive environments and reports that long-term reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following remain major obstacles \cite{liu2023agentbench}. MINT similarly evaluates agents in multi-turn interaction with tools and language feedback, showing that single-turn ability does not necessarily transfer to multi-turn settings \cite{wang2023mint}. In multi-turn tasks, the agent must remember prior constraints, update its plan after feedback, and maintain consistency across the conversation. This means that evaluation must consider the conversation as a trajectory rather than as a set of isolated responses.

Security work on prompt injection highlights a related problem: the agent must distinguish between trusted instructions, user messages, retrieved content, and tool outputs. Greshake et al. showed that LLM-integrated applications can be compromised by indirect prompt injection, where malicious instructions are embedded in external content and later processed by the model \cite{greshake2023not}. This is particularly relevant to agents that browse the web, retrieve documents, or process user-provided files. In these systems, an output can be fluent and task-relevant while still violating hidden constraints, leaking information, or following instructions from an untrusted source. The evaluation target therefore includes not only task success but also policy adherence and instruction hierarchy.

A further difficulty is non-determinism. Even with the same prompt and tools, stochastic decoding, tool latency, and slight contextual differences can produce different trajectories. Self-consistency uses this property to improve reasoning accuracy, but in production-like settings it becomes a reliability problem. A system that succeeds once but fails under repeated trials may not be dependable enough for deployment. This motivates metrics that measure consistency over repeated attempts, such as the \textit{pass}$^k$ metric introduced in $\tau$-bench for agent reliability \cite{yao2024taubench}. Repetition is therefore not only an experimental convenience; it is a way to expose instability in agent behaviour.

These failure modes imply that agent evaluation needs several forms of evidence. The evaluator may need to inspect the final natural-language response, the sequence of messages, the tools selected, the arguments passed, the observations received, and the final state of the environment. In conventional software engineering, these would be treated as different observables of a system under test. For LLM agents, the same perspective is increasingly necessary, because the behaviour that matters is distributed across the language model and the surrounding software environment.

A useful way to summarize this problem is to distinguish between \emph{answer correctness}, \emph{trajectory correctness}, and \emph{state correctness}. Answer correctness asks whether the final response is acceptable to the user. Trajectory correctness asks whether the agent followed an appropriate process: whether it asked for missing information, selected suitable tools, used observations rather than inventing facts, and avoided unnecessary actions. State correctness asks whether the external world after the episode satisfies the intended postconditions. These three notions can diverge. A customer-service agent might send a reassuring final response while failing to update an order; a web agent might reach the correct page through a fragile or unsafe sequence of actions; a coding agent might produce a patch that appears plausible but breaks unrelated tests. This separation helps explain why recent agent benchmarks increasingly expose logs, traces, and state transitions as part of their evaluation artefacts.

Another way to see the difference is through the cost of a mistake. In ordinary text generation, an incorrect sentence can often be corrected in the next turn. In a stateful agent, an incorrect action may change a database, send a message, place an order, or consume a limited resource. Even if the agent later apologizes or produces the right explanation, the environment may already be wrong. This is why the literature around tool use places increasing emphasis on side effects, abstention, and reversibility. It also explains why some agent evaluations include collateral-damage checks: completing the main task is not enough if the agent achieved it by disturbing unrelated state.

\section{General LLM Evaluation and Its Limits}
\label{sec:bg-general-eval}

Before considering agent-specific benchmarks, it is useful to understand the broader evolution of LLM evaluation. General language-model benchmarks provide standardized comparisons across models by measuring knowledge, reasoning, and natural-language understanding under fixed input--output conditions. MMLU, for example, measures performance across many academic and professional domains \cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}. These benchmarks are valuable because they make model progress visible and reproducible, but they generally evaluate static inputs and final outputs. They do not test whether a model can act over a sequence of steps, call tools correctly, or preserve external state.

HELM addressed part of this limitation by framing evaluation as a coverage problem across scenarios and metrics \cite{liang2022helm}. Rather than reporting only a single accuracy number, HELM evaluates models across dimensions such as robustness, fairness, calibration, toxicity, and efficiency. This multi-metric view is important because it resists the temptation to treat language-model quality as one scalar. A model can be accurate but poorly calibrated, efficient but unsafe, or robust on some prompt variations but brittle on others. The same principle applies even more strongly to agents: an agent may be successful according to a final-state metric while being inefficient, risky, or policy-violating along the way.

\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]

  \label{fig:helm-multi-metric}
\end{figure}

The rise of open-ended chat models also made reference-based metrics less adequate. Metrics such as exact match, BLEU, and ROUGE assume that good outputs overlap with a reference answer, but many valid responses may use different wording or structure. LLM-as-a-judge methods attempt to solve this by using a strong model to evaluate another model's output. G-Eval uses GPT-4 with rubric-like prompts and chain-of-thought style evaluation to score natural-language generation outputs, reporting stronger correlation with human judgments than many traditional metrics \cite{liu2023geval}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/geval_pipeline.png}
  \caption{The G-Eval pipeline: evaluation criteria and automatically generated chain-of-thought steps define a judge prompt, which scores the target output via a form-filling step and a probability-weighted aggregate score \cite{liu2023geval}.}
  \label{fig:geval-pipeline}
\end{figure}

MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena popularized pairwise and model-judge evaluation for conversational assistants, while also studying the limitations and biases of such judges \cite{zheng2023judging}. Other pairwise evaluation methods similarly use model comparisons for instruction-following evaluation, often with additional controls for judge biases.

These approaches are useful because they scale better than human evaluation and can handle open-ended natural language. However, they have important limitations for agent testing. A model judge can assess whether a final answer sounds helpful, but it may not reliably know whether the agent called the correct tool, whether a database was updated correctly, or whether a hidden domain rule was violated. It may also be biased toward verbose responses, familiar model styles, or plausible explanations. In a tool-using environment, a natural-language judge is therefore best seen as one possible oracle among several, not as a complete substitute for executable checks.

RAG evaluation frameworks illustrate a more component-oriented alternative. RAGAS decomposes RAG evaluation into dimensions such as context relevance, faithfulness, and answer relevance, allowing a system to be evaluated without always requiring a reference answer \cite{es2023ragas}. ARES similarly evaluates RAG systems along component dimensions, using synthetic data, lightweight judges, and a small amount of human annotation to improve reliability \cite{saadfalcon2023ares}. These frameworks are important because they show that a pipeline should be evaluated at the level of its components and evidence flow. Nevertheless, RAG evaluation is still narrower than full agent evaluation. A RAG system usually retrieves documents and generates an answer; an agent may read and write state, use multiple tools, interact with users, and make decisions whose effects persist beyond the final message.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/aries_pipeline.png}
  \caption{The ARES pipeline: synthetic queries are generated from in-domain passages, lightweight LLM judges are trained on a small human-validated set, and RAG systems are ranked with prediction-powered inference and confidence intervals \cite{saadfalcon2023ares}.}
  \label{fig:ares-pipeline}
\end{figure}

The broader lesson is that scalar metrics are useful but incomplete. A scalar score can support ranking and regression tracking, but it often hides the reason for failure. In an agent system, the practical debugging question is not merely whether the score decreased, but where the failure occurred: in the model's interpretation of the instruction, the tool choice, the tool arguments, the response to an observation, the final output, or the side effects on the environment. This motivates evaluation methods that preserve traces and expose structured failure information rather than collapsing everything into a single number.

This does not mean that scalar metrics should be abandoned. Benchmarks still need aggregate numbers in order to compare models, track progress, and communicate results. The issue is that the aggregate should be treated as a summary over a richer set of observations, not as the observation itself. A useful testing report should allow the reader to move from a high-level failure rate to concrete examples, and then from each example to the specific assertion or behavioural expectation that failed. This mirrors good practice in conventional software testing, where a test suite may report a percentage of passing tests, but developers debug by inspecting individual failing assertions, stack traces, logs, and inputs.

The granularity of the oracle should also match the granularity of the claim. If the claim is that a model is generally helpful in conversation, a judge score or preference comparison may be reasonable. If the claim is that an agent can safely operate a booking system, the evidence should include whether it selected the correct booking operation, used the right dates and passenger details, followed cancellation or refund policies, and left the database in the right state. This is why agent evaluation often requires multiple kinds of checks in the same scenario. The natural-language surface of the conversation, the structured tool interface, and the environment state each provide different evidence about correctness.

\section{Existing Evaluation Libraries and Observability Tooling}
\label{sec:existing_eval_libraries}

Alongside academic benchmarks, several practical libraries have emerged for evaluating LLM applications during development. These tools are useful because they move evaluation closer to the developer workflow: test cases can be rerun, prompts and models can be compared, and failures can be inspected more systematically than with ad hoc scripts. However, they also show that current tooling is still only partially suited to evaluating stateful, tool-using agents.

\subsection{OpenAI Evals}

OpenAI Evals is an open-source framework for evaluating LLMs and LLM systems \cite{openaiEvals}. It provides a general structure for defining datasets, running models on examples, and grading the resulting outputs. This is useful when developers want repeatable evaluation suites for comparing model versions or prompt changes. Evals can also use model-graded checks, where another LLM judges whether the output satisfies some rubric.

The limitation is that OpenAI Evals is still mainly organised around examples and graders. This works well for many single-turn tasks, but is less natural for agent evaluations where the important behaviour may be distributed across several turns, tool calls, and state changes. It can be extended with custom logic, but the framework does not provide a particularly direct scenario-level language for asserting, for example, that a user-facing output, a sequence of tool calls, and the resulting application state are all correct together.

\subsection{DeepEval and Ragas}

DeepEval and Ragas provide more application-oriented evaluation abstractions. DeepEval presents LLM evaluation in a unit-testing style and ships ready-made metrics, including G-Eval \cite{liu2023geval}, alongside measures for RAG, conversations, safety, and agentic behaviour \cite{deepevalDocs}. Its tool correctness metric evaluates whether expected tools were called, with options to consider input parameters and tool outputs \cite{deepevalDocs}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/geval_instantiation_code_snippet.png}
  \caption{G-Eval prompt instantiation using the DeepEval library: evaluation criteria and chain-of-thought instructions are composed into a judge prompt for scoring model outputs \cite{liu2023geval,deepevalDocs}.}
  \label{fig:geval-prompt}
\end{figure}

Ragas was originally designed for RAG evaluation, decomposing quality into dimensions such as faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision, and context recall \cite{es2023ragas,ragasDocs}. More recent versions also include agent metrics such as tool call accuracy, tool call F1, and agent goal accuracy \cite{ragasDocs}. Agent goal accuracy is a binary multi-turn metric: an evaluator LLM reads the full interaction trace, infers the outcome the agent reached, and judges whether it matches the user's goal---either against a provided reference outcome or by inferring the goal from the conversation \cite{ragasDocs}.

These libraries are valuable because they recognise that LLM systems should not only be judged by final-answer quality. However, their tool checks are still relatively metric-oriented. They are useful for comparing expected and actual tool calls, but they are less ergonomic when a developer wants to specify precise properties of a tool argument or a structured output. For example, checking that a particular tool argument contains the substring \texttt{urgent}, or that a nested structured output field satisfies a domain-specific predicate, is not usually a first-class assertion. It generally requires custom code, a custom metric, or reshaping the output into the format expected by the evaluator. This makes simple behavioural expectations feel heavier than ordinary software tests.

\subsection{Promptfoo}

Promptfoo is an open-source tool for evaluating and red-teaming LLM applications using declarative test cases \cite{promptfooDocs}. It supports prompt and model comparison, assertions, CI/CD workflows, caching, and multiple providers. It also includes deterministic assertions such as equality, substring containment, regex matching, JSON validation, and custom JavaScript or Python assertions \cite{promptfooDocs}. This makes it closer to traditional test-driven development than many purely metric-based evaluation frameworks.

Promptfoo is therefore one of the more practical tools for developer-led LLM testing. However, its main abstraction is still the prompt/application test case and its assertions. More complex agent checks, especially those involving multi-turn traces, tool-call arguments, structured outputs, and stateful side effects, can become awkward unless the developer writes custom assertion logic. It provides a useful assertion vocabulary, but it is less focused on representing a full agent scenario as a single object with output checks, tool checks, and state checks expressed together.

\subsection{Observability-Focused Tools}

A related group of tools focuses on observability rather than evaluation alone. LangSmith provides tracing, evaluation, prompt testing, feedback collection, and production monitoring for LLM applications \cite{langsmithDocs}. Langfuse similarly provides open-source application tracing, prompt management, evaluation, user feedback, and monitoring for LLM systems \cite{langfuseDocs}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/langfuse_example_trace.png}
  \caption{Example Langfuse trace for a RAG-style QA request: nested observations record retrieval (embedding and vector-store lookup), prompt retrieval, and final generation, with per-step latency, token usage, inputs, outputs, and cost \cite{langfuseDocs}.}
  \label{fig:langfuse-trace}
\end{figure}

These tools are important because agent failures are often hard to understand without seeing the full execution trace: the prompts, model responses, tool calls, tool outputs, intermediate steps, latency, and cost.

Observability tools are therefore highly complementary to evaluation libraries. They help developers inspect what happened during a run. However, observability does not by itself define what should have happened. A trace viewer can show that a tool was called, but the developer still needs a concise way to specify that the tool should only have been called after another condition, that its arguments should satisfy particular properties, that the final output should mention a required concept, and that the external state should end in a valid configuration.

\subsection{Gaps in Existing Libraries}

Existing libraries cover many important parts of the evaluation workflow, but they leave a gap for scenario-driven agent testing. Some tools are strong at scoring final outputs. Others provide RAG or tool-use metrics. Others provide trace observability and production monitoring. What is still missing is a developer-friendly way to define a complete agent scenario and attach explicit checks over the different surfaces of behaviour.

In particular, current tooling does not make it easy enough to combine output assertions, tool-call assertions, and state assertions in one coherent test. A developer should be able to express checks such as: the final response contains a required phrase, a particular tool was called with an argument satisfying a property, a forbidden tool was not called, and the resulting state matches the intended outcome. These checks should be easy to read, easy to modify, and should produce useful diagnostic output explaining which part of the scenario failed.

This is especially important for LLM agents because failures are often not visible in the final response alone. An agent may answer politely while calling the wrong tool, using the wrong argument, skipping a required step, or leaving the system in an invalid state. Conversely, a generic score may indicate failure without showing whether the problem was in the output, the tool trajectory, or the state transition. The main gap is therefore not simply the absence of metrics, but the absence of concise scenario-style evaluation tooling that treats the full agent trace as the object being tested.


\section{Tool-Use and Function-Calling Benchmarks}
\label{sec:bg-tool-benchmarks}

A first wave of tool-use benchmarks focused on whether LLMs could map natural-language instructions to API calls. API-Bank introduced a benchmark for tool-augmented LLMs involving API use in dialogue settings, with tasks that require understanding user intent, selecting APIs, and producing correct calls \cite{li2023apibank}. This work is important because it treats tool use as a concrete capability rather than as an informal extension of prompting. Once APIs are available, the model must comply with machine-readable constraints, not only with natural-language expectations.

ToolLLM and ToolBench scaled this idea to a much larger API space. Qin et al. collected more than 16,000 real-world APIs and generated instructions involving both single-tool and multi-tool scenarios \cite{qin2023toollm}. This reflects an important reality of deployed agents: they may have access to many tools, and the difficulty is not only calling a tool, but identifying the relevant subset of tools and composing them correctly. ToolLLM also introduced ToolEval as an automatic evaluator, showing that the evaluation problem grows with the tool space. A wrong call may be syntactically valid but semantically inappropriate, and a partially correct call chain may still fail the user's goal.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/toolbench_construction_pipeline.png}
  \caption{ToolBench construction and evaluation pipeline: APIs are collected from RapidAPI, instructions and solution paths are generated, an API retriever and ToolLLaMA are trained, and ToolEval scores the final answers \cite{qin2023toollm}.}
  \label{fig:toolbench-pipeline}
\end{figure}

StableToolBench later addressed reproducibility problems in large-scale tool-use evaluation. Guo et al. observed that benchmarks relying on real online APIs can be unstable because API status, outputs, and availability change over time \cite{guo2024stabletoolbench}. Their virtual API server and stable evaluation system illustrate a key engineering principle for agent evaluation: the environment must be controlled enough that differences in results can be attributed to the agent rather than to external noise. This is a recurring challenge in any benchmark or testing framework that exercises tools, because real tools are often stateful, non-deterministic, rate-limited, or unavailable.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/api_status_change_breakdown.png}
  \caption{Breakdown of live API call outcomes in StableToolBench: fewer than half of calls succeed, with parsing errors, connectivity failures, and authorisation issues dominating non-success cases \cite{guo2024stabletoolbench}.}
  \label{fig:stabletoolbench-api-status}
\end{figure}

BFCL provides a more recent and systematic view of function calling. It evaluates capabilities such as function selection, relevance detection, AST-based argument checking, executable calls, parallel calls, multi-turn interactions, and agentic scenarios \cite{patil2025bfcl}. Its importance lies in making tool-call correctness precise. A final response may be impossible to judge without knowing whether the call was syntactically valid, whether the arguments matched the schema, whether the right number of calls were made, and whether the model should have abstained. BFCL therefore captures a core idea for agent evaluation: the intermediate interface between the model and the software environment must itself be tested.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/ast_based_function_evaluation.png}
  \caption{AST-based function-call evaluation in BFCL: the generated call is parsed and checked for function name, required and optional parameters, types, and values against the reference \cite{patil2025bfcl}.}
  \label{fig:bfcl-ast-eval}
\end{figure}

Despite their usefulness, function-calling benchmarks still cover only part of the agent problem. Many tasks require more than producing the correct call in isolation. The agent may need to ask the user for missing information, interpret tool results, update long-lived state, and avoid unwanted side effects. In such settings, evaluating individual calls is necessary but not sufficient. The next step in the literature is therefore toward executable environments that can measure whether the sequence of calls actually achieved the intended goal.

Nevertheless, these function-calling benchmarks introduce several ideas that remain essential in more complex settings. One is schema awareness: the model must respect the types and required fields of the function interface. Another is semantic argument matching: two arguments may be syntactically similar but operationally different, such as a pickup date versus a return date. A third is negative capability: the model should know when no available function is appropriate. These are not cosmetic details. They are precisely the points at which a language model becomes coupled to ordinary software, and therefore the points at which ordinary software-style testing becomes relevant.

They also show why tool traces should be normalized before evaluation. Different agent frameworks may represent tool calls as chat messages, JSON blobs, callback events, or framework-specific objects. If evaluation is tied to one raw representation, it becomes fragile and difficult to compare across systems. By contrast, benchmarks such as BFCL implicitly evaluate a more abstract notion of a function call: name, arguments, ordering, execution result, and sometimes abstention. This abstraction is a useful lesson for agent testing generally. The trace should be rich enough to preserve the behaviour of the agent, but normalized enough that assertions can be written over stable semantic events rather than over incidental framework details.

\section{Executable and Stateful Agent Benchmarks}
\label{sec:bg-stateful-benchmarks}

  \caption{AgentBench evaluates an LLM-as-agent across eight interactive environments spanning operating systems, databases, knowledge graphs, games, household simulation, lateral-thinking puzzles, and web tasks \cite{liu2023agentbench}.}
  \label{fig:agentbench-overview}
\end{figure}

AgentBench is one of the broad early benchmarks that explicitly evaluates LLMs as agents rather than as static text predictors \cite{liu2023agentbench}. It includes multiple interactive environments and emphasizes reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following over multi-turn interactions. The significance of AgentBench is that it shifts the unit of evaluation from a single prompt to an episode. The model must act repeatedly, observe the consequences, and continue toward a goal. This already resembles software-system testing more than traditional NLP benchmarking.

WebArena adds realism by evaluating agents in reproducible web environments \cite{zhou2023webarena}. It includes tasks across websites such as shopping, social discussion, content management, and map-based interactions. The benchmark reports a substantial gap between strong LLM agents and human performance, showing that realistic web tasks remain difficult even for capable models. WebArena matters because the state of the website is part of the task. A response that says the task is complete is not sufficient; the environment must actually reflect the correct outcome.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/webagent_environment.png}
  \caption{WebArena: an agent acts on self-hosted websites and tool sites with programmatic validators that check functional success or failure from the resulting environment state \cite{zhou2023webarena}.}
  \label{fig:webarena-environment}
\end{figure}

SWE-bench extends executable evaluation into software engineering. Jimenez et al. evaluate whether models can resolve real GitHub issues by modifying code and passing tests \cite{jimenez2024swebench}. Although SWE-bench is not primarily a conversational tool-use benchmark, it is highly relevant because it uses external execution as the oracle. A model's patch is judged by whether it passes tests in a repository, not by whether its explanation sounds plausible. This is a strong example of the broader movement toward functional evaluation: when the task has real side effects, the best evidence of success is often executable behaviour.

Planning benchmarks provide another angle. PlanBench evaluates LLMs on planning and reasoning about change, focusing on whether models can reason about preconditions, effects, and goal states \cite{valmeekam2023planbench}. This matters because many agents implicitly perform planning even when they are not using a classical planner. They must decide which actions are possible, which actions are necessary, and how actions change the world. Failures in planning often appear as missing steps, impossible tool calls, or incorrect assumptions about state.

AppWorld is especially important for stateful agent evaluation. It provides a controllable environment of day-to-day apps with hundreds of APIs and uses programmatic state-based unit tests to check whether tasks were completed correctly \cite{trivedi2024appworld}. Crucially, AppWorld also checks for collateral damage: an agent should not satisfy the main goal by making unrelated or harmful changes elsewhere. This illustrates a central limitation of final-answer evaluation. A textual answer can say that the right action was performed, but only a state-based check can determine whether the external system was actually updated correctly and safely.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/app_world_engine_and_benchmark.png}
  \caption{AppWorld Engine and Benchmark: simulated apps and a local database support a stateful assistant whose success is verified by EvalTests over the initial and final environment states \cite{trivedi2024appworld}.}
  \label{fig:appworld-engine}
\end{figure}

$\tau$-bench focuses on dynamic tool-agent-user interaction in realistic domains such as retail and airline tasks \cite{yao2024taubench}. It uses simulated users, domain-specific APIs, and policy guidelines, then evaluates success by comparing the final database state with an annotated goal state. It also introduces \textit{pass}$^k$ to measure how consistently an agent succeeds over repeated trials. This combination of simulated interaction, tool use, domain policy, final-state evaluation, and repeated runs is highly representative of where agent evaluation is heading. It treats an LLM agent less like a text generator and more like an unreliable controller in a stateful environment.

ToolSandbox makes the same point from another direction. Lu et al. argue that previous tool-use evaluations often rely on stateless services, single-turn prompts, or off-policy trajectories, and introduce a benchmark with stateful tool execution, implicit dependencies between tools, on-policy user simulation, and milestone-based evaluation \cite{lu2024toolsandbox}. This is important because many real tasks cannot be evaluated only at the end. An agent may need to satisfy intermediate conditions, obtain missing information, or respect dependencies that are not obvious from a single tool schema. Milestone evaluation allows the process itself to be inspected rather than treating the trajectory as a black box.

The same process-level argument follows from the milestone and state-based designs of ToolSandbox, AppWorld and $\tau$-bench. In all three settings, a final answer is not enough to explain the behaviour of the system: the evaluator must know which intermediate steps were taken, whether the required information was collected, and whether any state changes were unnecessary or harmful \cite{lu2024toolsandbox,trivedi2024appworld,yao2024taubench}. This reinforces the need to preserve and evaluate traces. An agent evaluation that stores only final success or failure loses the information most useful for debugging. A process-level view asks which step failed, whether the failure was recoverable, and whether the final outcome hides unsafe behaviour along the way.

Together, these benchmarks show a clear trend. The field is moving from static final-output benchmarks toward executable, stateful, interactive, and repeated evaluations. This does not make earlier NLP benchmarks obsolete, but it changes their role. General benchmarks measure broad model capability; agent benchmarks measure whether that capability survives when embedded into a system with tools, users, policies, and side effects.

\section{Test Oracles, Specifications and Structured Evidence}
\label{sec:bg-oracles}

The software-testing literature provides useful language for understanding the evaluation problem. A test oracle is a mechanism for deciding whether the observed behaviour of a system is correct. Barr et al. describe the oracle problem as one of the central difficulties in software testing: generating inputs is not enough if there is no reliable way to determine whether the output is right \cite{barr2015oracle}. LLM systems intensify this problem because outputs are open-ended, context-dependent, and often partially correct. A strict string comparison is usually too brittle, while an unconstrained natural-language judgment may be too vague.

For LLM agents, there is rarely a single oracle. Instead, different aspects of behaviour require different checks. A final answer may be judged by semantic relevance or factuality. A structured output may be checked against a schema. A tool call may be checked by function name, arguments, order, and result. A stateful environment may be checked by invariants or postconditions. A security property may be checked by whether the agent refused an unsafe request or ignored untrusted instructions. This suggests that agent evaluation should be built from multiple smaller oracles rather than from one universal score.

Structured evidence is therefore essential. If an evaluator records only that a scenario failed, the developer still has to reconstruct the interaction manually. If the evaluator records the conversation, tool calls, arguments, observations, and state checks, then the failure becomes diagnosable. This mirrors conventional testing practice, where assertion failures ideally identify the expected value, the actual value, and the location of the mismatch. In agent systems, the corresponding location may be a turn, a tool call, a nested argument path, or a postcondition over state.

The need for structured evidence also explains why LLM-as-a-judge evaluation should be used carefully. LLM judges are flexible and can evaluate qualities that are difficult to encode as deterministic predicates. However, they are less reliable for checking precise machine-level facts unless those facts are provided explicitly and the rubric is narrow. A judge might be suitable for assessing whether a final answer is empathetic or whether a summary captures key points. It is less suitable as the only mechanism for verifying whether a JSON argument matched a schema, a database row was updated, or an API was called exactly once. A robust evaluation setup should therefore combine deterministic assertions, structured matchers, executable state checks, and, where appropriate, LLM-based rubrics.

Metamorphic testing provides another useful idea. Chen et al. proposed testing systems by checking relations between multiple executions, especially when exact expected outputs are hard to know \cite{chen1998metamorphic}. For LLM agents, this can mean checking that harmless paraphrases of a user request preserve the same intended outcome, that adding irrelevant context does not change a decision, or that a tool result with the same semantic content leads to the same action. These relations are not exact answers, but they define properties that should hold across variations. This perspective connects LLM robustness testing to a broader tradition of specification-based testing.

\section{Property-Based Testing, Fuzzing and Shrinking}
\label{sec:bg-testing-lineage}

Property-based testing is a natural predecessor for modern generative evaluation. QuickCheck introduced a lightweight approach in which a developer specifies properties that should hold for many randomly generated inputs \cite{claessen2000quickcheck}. Instead of writing only a few examples by hand, the testing system explores a larger input space and reports counterexamples when a property fails. This approach is useful whenever manually enumerating all important cases is impractical. LLM agents have the same problem: the space of possible user messages, conversation paths, tool observations, and edge cases is too large to cover with only hand-written scripts.

The key insight from property-based testing is that test generation and test checking are separate concerns. Random or generative methods can propose diverse cases, but correctness still depends on properties or oracles. In an LLM setting, generated conversations are useful only if they are paired with assertions about expected behaviour, such as required tool usage, forbidden side effects, or acceptable final responses. Otherwise, generation produces transcripts but not tests. This distinction is important because many LLM evaluation approaches generate prompts at scale but rely on weak or purely qualitative assessment. Property-based testing suggests a more disciplined pattern: generate many cases, check explicit properties, and preserve failures as counterexamples.

Shrinking is the companion idea. When QuickCheck finds a failing random input, it attempts to reduce it to a smaller failing example. Delta Debugging formalized this broader debugging problem by automatically simplifying failure-inducing inputs while preserving the failure \cite{zeller2002simplifying}. This is valuable because large failing cases are often hard to understand. A long conversation may contain many irrelevant turns; a prompt may contain unnecessary wording; a sequence of API calls may include steps that are not actually required to trigger the bug. A reduced counterexample is far more useful because it identifies the minimal circumstances under which the system fails.

In LLM agent testing, shrinking is more subtle than in ordinary input testing. A conversation is not just a string; it is an interactive sequence where removing one turn may change all later turns. A shrinker must preserve enough context for the failure to remain meaningful, and it must distinguish between reproducing the same failure and producing a different failure. Nevertheless, the conceptual value is the same as in delta debugging: reduce developer effort by isolating the failure-inducing part of a complex interaction.

Fuzzing provides another testing lineage. Traditional fuzzing repeatedly mutates inputs to discover crashes, security vulnerabilities, or unexpected behaviours. LLM-specific fuzzing work has adapted this idea to prompts and safety failures. GPTFuzzer uses seed prompts, mutation operators, and a judgment model to generate jailbreak prompts automatically \cite{yu2023gptfuzzer}. FuzzLLM takes a more structured approach \cite{yao2023fuzzllm}. It classifies jailbreak attacks into three base types---role play (altering conversational context through a scenario), output constraint (redirecting attention at the output level), and privilege escalation (inducing the model to break its restrictions)---and combines them into seven template classes, from single-base attacks through pairwise and triple combinations. Each jailbreak prompt is built from three components: a fuzzing template that carries the attack class, a constraint set that shapes how the attack is phrased, and a set of policy-violating questions. Templates can be paraphrased to increase diversity before being sent to a target model; a separate label model then acts as an oracle, checking whether the response violates usage policy.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fuzz_llm_pipeline.png}
  \caption{FuzzLLM pipeline: prompts are assembled from base and combo-class templates with constraints and policy-violating questions, executed against a target model, and automatically classified by a label-model oracle \cite{yao2023fuzzllm}.}
  \label{fig:fuzzllm-pipeline}
\end{figure}

Although these papers focus mainly on safety and red teaming, they show that systematic variation plus an oracle can discover failures at scale---a pattern that extends beyond jailbreak testing. The limitation of safety-focused prompt fuzzing is that it usually targets a narrow failure predicate, such as whether a model produces disallowed content. Agent testing requires broader predicates. A generated user behaviour may be interesting because it triggers a wrong tool call, an unnecessary clarification, a policy violation, or a state error. This means that fuzzing for agents should be guided by domain-specific assertions and should preserve enough trace information to diagnose the failure. The value of fuzzing is not merely that it finds more failures, but that it explores behaviours that manual scripts may miss.

A further issue is distribution. Randomly generated user messages are not automatically useful. If generation is too unconstrained, many cases will be unrealistic or irrelevant; if it is too constrained, it may only reproduce the manual examples that were already known. Property-based testing addresses the analogous problem through generators that encode domain structure. For LLM agents, the generator may need to encode user personas, task variants, missing information, ambiguous phrasing, or adversarial but plausible behaviour. This suggests that generative testing is most valuable when it is scenario-aware: the generated variation should remain inside the domain of the task while still exploring corners that a human tester may not have enumerated.

The same applies to shrinking. A minimal failing interaction is not necessarily the shortest possible string; it is the simplest interaction that still demonstrates the same behavioural failure. If a shrink step removes a turn and the agent now fails for a different reason, the counterexample may be smaller but less useful. This is why failure signatures and behavioural equivalence matter in agent debugging. The goal is not only to make the transcript short, but to preserve the diagnostic meaning of the original failure. This is one of the places where classical delta debugging ideas must be adapted to conversational and stateful systems.

Regression testing completes the testing loop. Once a failure is found and understood, it should be turned into a stable test that can be rerun after changes. This is especially important for LLM systems because changes to prompts, models, tools, or orchestration code can fix one behaviour while breaking another. In conventional software, regression tests protect against reintroducing known bugs. In LLM agents, regression tests are harder because outputs may remain stochastic, but the principle is the same: previously observed failure modes should become part of the evaluation suite. A good regression captures the behavioural condition that matters, not necessarily every token of the original transcript.

\section{Optimization Frameworks as Adjacent Work}
\label{sec:bg-adjacent-optimisation}

Prompt and pipeline optimization is related to agent testing, but it is not the same problem. DSPy provides a declarative framework for composing language-model calls and optimizing prompts or demonstrations against a metric \cite{khattab2023dspy}. MIPRO extends this line by optimizing instructions and demonstrations in multi-stage language-model programs \cite{opsahlong2024optimizing}. GEPA uses reflective prompt evolution and natural-language feedback to search for improved prompts \cite{agrawal2025gepa}. Prompt optimization with human feedback similarly explores how human preferences can guide prompt search when scalar metrics are unreliable \cite{lin2024prompt}. These systems are relevant because they show that prompts and LLM pipelines can be treated as tunable artefacts rather than fixed strings.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/prompt_optimisers_across_rollouts.png}
  \caption{Learning curves for GEPA, MIPROv2, and GRPO on HotpotQA and IFBench: prompt optimizers reach higher validation scores with far fewer rollouts than GRPO, while test-set stars show held-out performance \cite{agrawal2025gepa,opsahlong2024optimizing}. The improvement is entirely defined by the chosen evaluation metric---optimization is powerful, but only as good as the signal it optimizes.}
  \label{fig:prompt-optimisers-rollouts}
\end{figure}

However, optimization frameworks assume that there is an objective to optimize. In well-defined question-answering tasks, that objective might be exact-match accuracy or F1. In RAG tasks, it might be answer faithfulness or context relevance. In open-ended agent settings, the objective is often harder to write down. A domain expert may care about tool ordering, safety constraints, side effects, and conversational quality simultaneously. Optimizing a single scalar proxy can therefore hide important trade-offs. This is why the testing perspective remains necessary even when optimization is available. Testing does not only ask which prompt scores highest; it asks what behaviour occurred, why it failed, and whether known failures remain fixed.

The relationship between the two areas is complementary. A testing framework can produce the evidence and regression cases that later guide optimization. An optimization framework can search for prompts or policies that perform better on those tests. But the literature suggests that without good evaluation artefacts, optimization can become misdirected. A system may improve on a judge score while becoming less safe, less interpretable, or less reliable under interaction. For agentic systems, optimization should therefore be downstream of structured evaluation rather than a substitute for it.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:bg-synthesis}

The literature reviewed in this chapter shows a clear progression. Transformers and scaling produced instruction-following models that can be controlled through prompts \cite{vaswani2017attention,brown2020language,ouyang2022training}. Prompting and reasoning work showed that model behaviour is sensitive to instructions, intermediate reasoning, and repeated sampling \cite{wei2022chain,kojima2022large,wang2022self}. RAG, ReAct, Toolformer, and API-focused systems then embedded LLMs into pipelines and environments where actions, tools, and observations matter \cite{lewis2020retrieval,yao2023react,schick2023toolformer,patil2023gorilla}. As a result, the evaluation problem changed from assessing final text to assessing the behaviour of an executable system.

General evaluation work such as HELM, G-Eval, MT-Bench, RAGAS, and ARES provides useful methods for measuring broad language quality and component behaviour \cite{liang2022helm,liu2023geval,zheng2023judging,es2023ragas,saadfalcon2023ares}. Yet the agent-specific literature shows that these methods are not enough on their own. Tool-use benchmarks such as API-Bank, ToolBench, StableToolBench, and BFCL make tool-call correctness explicit \cite{li2023apibank,qin2023toollm,guo2024stabletoolbench,patil2025bfcl}. Stateful benchmarks such as WebArena, AppWorld, $\tau$-bench, and ToolSandbox go further by evaluating agents inside environments whose state can be inspected \cite{zhou2023webarena,trivedi2024appworld,yao2024taubench,lu2024toolsandbox}. These benchmarks provide strong evidence that realistic agent evaluation must consider traces, tools, state, and repeated reliability.

Software-testing research supplies the complementary methodological foundation. The oracle problem explains why correctness is difficult to determine for complex outputs \cite{barr2015oracle}. Property-based testing shows how generated cases can be paired with explicit properties \cite{claessen2000quickcheck}. Delta debugging explains why failing cases should be minimized into useful counterexamples \cite{zeller2002simplifying}. LLM fuzzing demonstrates that automated test generation is already being applied to language-model behaviours \cite{yu2023gptfuzzer,yao2023fuzzllm}. Together, these ideas point toward a view of LLM agents as software systems that require scenario specifications, structured oracles, trace-level evidence, failure reduction, and regression suites.

This view also clarifies the role of human judgement. Human feedback remains essential when correctness is subjective, domain-specific, or underspecified, but the literature suggests that human judgement should be used where it adds the most information. A human may be excellent at explaining why a response was unhelpful, why a policy interpretation was wrong, or why a clarification question was necessary. A deterministic checker may be better at verifying that a function was called with a required identifier or that a final state satisfies a postcondition. Strong evaluation practice therefore does not require choosing between human judgement, LLM judges, and deterministic tests. It requires composing them into a layered evaluation process.

The resulting research gap is not simply a lack of another leaderboard. Many leaderboards are designed to compare models under fixed tasks. The harder engineering problem is how to help developers specify, run, inspect, minimize, and preserve behavioural tests for their own agents. Recent benchmarks provide evidence that agents fail in realistic environments; software-testing ideas provide mechanisms for finding and managing failures; evaluation research provides judge and metric techniques for ambiguous outputs. The intersection of these areas defines the background for practical agent reliability work.

This background therefore motivates a testing-oriented view of LLM agents. The key challenge is not only to build more capable agents, nor only to optimize prompts, but to make agent behaviour observable and checkable. A mature evaluation approach must capture final outputs, intermediate actions, and environment effects; it must handle stochastic variation through repeated execution; it must use both deterministic and model-based oracles where appropriate; and it must turn discovered failures into reusable tests. This is the setting in which scenario-driven testing, structured assertions, fuzzing, shrinking, and regression extraction become natural engineering responses to the reliability problems exposed by recent agent literature.
