% \section{Domain-Specific Tasks without Clear Metrics}

% The perhaps more important and challenging part of the evaluation concerns tasks where correctness is subjective, contextual, or highly domain-specific. In these settings, there is often no single correct answer, human annotators may reasonably disagree, and automated metrics provide at best a weak proxy for quality. Yet these are precisely the tasks where LLMs are most commonly deployed in practice, and where prompt design has the greatest influence on system behaviour. Evaluating prompt optimisation in this setting therefore requires moving beyond accuracy-based metrics and confronting the problem of how quality should be defined and measured.
% \newline

% One class of suitable benchmarks comes from subjective question answering datasets such as SubjQA, which contain review-based questions across domains including electronics, hotels, restaurants, and movies \cite{SubQA_dataset}. Questions such as “Is this hotel quiet?” or “Is the sound quality good?” admit multiple valid answers grounded in the same evidence, but differ in tone, emphasis, and stance. In these tasks, there is no canonical gold output; instead, quality depends on a particular set of preferences, such as favouring boutique hotels over large chains or valuing atmosphere over price. Prompt optimisation can therefore be evaluated by asking whether an optimisation method can align model outputs with such preferences. Traditional automated metrics and optimisation methods are not designed to capture or optimise for these subjective notions of quality.
% \newline

% In addition to existing datasets, the evaluation may include synthetic, specification-driven tasks, where the objective is not to produce a unique correct answer but to satisfy a set of explicit criteria, such as required elements, structure, or constraints. In such tasks, prompt optimisation can be evaluated by measuring how well outputs adhere to the specification. A simple metric could be the percentage of points in the specification that have been satisfied, and the use of this scalar metric means that a comparison can be made against existing optimisation methods.
% \newline

% Evaluation in this setting could therefore rely on a combination of approaches, including blind pairwise comparisons or LLM-based judges that check outputs against defined criteria/specifications. This enables prompt optimisation methods to be evaluated in settings where traditional automated metrics fail to capture the nuances of a task, and where improvements in prompt quality are reflected in better adherence to task intent rather than higher accuracy scores.
